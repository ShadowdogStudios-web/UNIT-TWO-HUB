Chunk 1

The Homeland

Where the Rock River flows into the Mississippi — at the place we now call Rock Island, Illinois — stood a great town called Saukenuk. It was the home of the Sauk (also spelled Sac) people and their close allies, the Meskwaki (the Fox). Saukenuk was one of the largest Native towns anywhere in North America, with lodges, council houses, and cornfields that stretched for miles along the river. Every spring the Sauk returned from their winter hunt to plant corn in the same fields their families had farmed for generations. This land was the center of their world.

Chunk 2

The Broken Treaty

In 1804, a few Sauk men traveled to the city of St. Louis. While they were there, an American official named William Henry Harrison got them to sign a treaty. On paper, that treaty gave away almost all Sauk and Fox land east of the Mississippi River — about 50 million acres — for a small yearly payment. But there was a serious problem: the men who signed had not been sent to sell land, and they had no power to speak for the whole nation. Many Sauk said the signers had been given alcohol and did not understand what they were agreeing to. A war leader named Black Hawk believed the treaty was a fraud and that the land still belonged to his people. The United States, however, treated the treaty as the law.

Chunk 3

Two Leaders

As American settlers pushed closer, the Sauk had to decide what to do, and two leaders saw the future in very different ways. One was Keokuk, called “the Watchful Fox.” Keokuk believed the Sauk could not win a war against the United States, so he led many of his people west across the Mississippi to keep the peace and stay alive. The other was Black Hawk. He had fought beside the British against the Americans in the War of 1812, and he refused to give up Saukenuk. The group of Sauk who followed him became known as the “British Band.” By 1831 the U.S. Army had forced Black Hawk’s people across the river — but Black Hawk was not ready to stay away.

Chunk 4

The Return

In April 1832, Black Hawk led about 1,100 of his people back across the Mississippi River into Illinois. This was not an army marching to attack. Most of the group were women, children, and elders who simply wanted to return home and plant corn. Black Hawk hoped that other Native nations and his old British allies would join him. Almost none did. Now he was deep inside Illinois, on land the state claimed as its own, with very little food and the United States Army gathering behind him.

Chunk 5

Illinois Reacts, and the Shooting Starts

The return of the British Band frightened settlers, and the governor of Illinois, John Reynolds, called out the state militia. Thousands of Illinois volunteers rode out beside the U.S. Army. One of those volunteers was a 23-year-old store clerk from New Salem, Illinois, named Abraham Lincoln, who was elected captain of his company. On May 14, 1832, Black Hawk realized no help was coming and sent riders out under a white flag to surrender. But nervous, untrained militia fired on the truce party and killed several men. Black Hawk’s warriors fought back and chased off a much larger militia force. People mocked the retreat as “Stillman’s Run.” Because of that mistake, a surrender turned into a war.

Chunk 6

The Retreat and the Bad Axe

Now Black Hawk’s people had to run. They fled north, out of Illinois and into the land that is now Wisconsin, with soldiers close behind and hunger and sickness killing many along the way. In late July, Black Hawk fought a skillful battle at Wisconsin Heights so his people could cross a river and keep moving. It only slowed the end. On August 1 and 2, 1832, the starving band reached the Mississippi at a place called the Bad Axe. As they tried to surrender and cross to safety, an armed steamboat called the Warrior and pursuing soldiers opened fire on them — men, women, and children. Of the roughly 1,100 people who had crossed into Illinois in the spring, only about 150 survived. The Black Hawk War was over.

Chunk 7

Aftermath, and Why Illinois Mattered

Black Hawk was captured and taken to eastern cities, where crowds came to see him and he even met President Andrew Jackson. He then told the story of his life to a writer, and Life of Black Hawk (1833) became the first Native American autobiography ever published in the United States. The war’s effects on Illinois were huge. It broke the last organized Native resistance in the state and threw northern Illinois open to a flood of new settlers; within a few years, Chicago grew from a tiny village into a booming city. And a remarkable group of men passed through this Illinois campaign: a future president of the United States (Lincoln), a future president of the Confederacy (Jefferson Davis), and a future president and general (Zachary Taylor). A fifteen-week war fought on Illinois soil had helped shape the future of the whole nation.
